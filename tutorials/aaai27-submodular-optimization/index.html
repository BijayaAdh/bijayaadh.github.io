01 / Overview

Submodularity for
AI decision making

Submodular optimization provides a powerful framework for AI decision problems in which the benefit of an action decreases as more actions are taken. This tutorial introduces the foundations of submodularity and explores advances that extend its reach from selecting sets to allocating multiple units of a resource, handling richer constraints, and optimizing objectives that exhibit diminishing returns only approximately.

We begin with classical submodular set functions, greedy algorithms, and approximation guarantees. We then examine diminishing-returns (DR) submodularity over integer lattices, with an emphasis on multi-unit resource allocation, and briefly connect these ideas to continuous domains. Topics include reductions from integer-lattice problems to set-submodular optimization and scalable algorithms under cardinality and knapsack constraints. We next discuss how curvature captures additional structure and can strengthen approximation guarantees, followed by approximate and weak submodularity and composite objectives combining submodular and supermodular components.

Throughout the tutorial, examples from data and feature selection, influence propagation, sensing and coverage, recommendation, and resource allocation for social good will connect theoretical ideas to AI applications.

Before you attend

Who is this for?

Researchers, practitioners, and graduate students in AI, machine learning, optimization, and related fields.

Prerequisites

Basic familiarity with algorithms, discrete optimization, and standard mathematical notation.

Exposure to greedy algorithms, approximation algorithms, or constrained optimization is helpful but not required.

No prior knowledge of submodular optimization is assumed. We introduce the necessary definitions and intuition.

What you will take away

  1. Recognize the structure

    Identify diminishing returns in AI objectives and distinguish set, integer-lattice, and continuous formulations.

  2. Build the model

    Express selection and multi-unit allocation decisions with appropriate objectives and resource constraints.

  3. Choose an algorithm

    Connect problem structure to scalable methods and understand what their approximation guarantees depend on.

02 / Program

From foundations to applications

The lecture connects algorithmic ideas to concrete AI modeling examples.

  1. Motivation and AI applications

    Where diminishing returns arise, why they matter, and how submodularity supports decision making under limited resources.

  2. Foundations of submodular optimization

    Set functions, marginal gains, monotonicity, greedy algorithms, and approximation guarantees.

  3. DR-submodularity and multi-unit allocation

    Diminishing returns over integer lattices, the distinction from lattice submodularity, and a brief connection to continuous domains.

  4. Reductions and scalable algorithms

    Connections to set-submodular optimization, direct lattice methods, and cardinality and knapsack constraints.

  5. Curvature and stronger guarantees

    How the strength of diminishing returns captures additional structure and informs approximation analysis.

  6. Approximate submodularity and composite objectives

    Weak and approximate diminishing returns, the submodularity ratio, and objectives combining submodular and supermodular components.

  7. AI modeling examples

    Resource allocation, data and feature selection, influence propagation, sensing and coverage, recommendation, and social good.

  8. Open directions and discussion

    Bringing the frameworks together, identifying research questions, and discussing applications with attendees.

04 / Presenter

Bijaya Adhikari

Associate Professor · University of Iowa

Bijaya Adhikari is an associate professor in the Department of Computer Science at the University of Iowa and a member of its Computational Epidemiology Group. His research focuses on data mining and machine learning for healthcare and public health, including graph learning and time-series analysis for clinical prediction, disease surveillance, and infection prevention. He received his PhD in Computer Science from Virginia Tech, advised by B. Aditya Prakash, and his bachelor’s degree in Computer Engineering from Vistula University in Warsaw, Poland. He is a recipient of an NSF CAREER Award.
